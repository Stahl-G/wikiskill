# Verify the Theorem, Not the Survivor

Reconstruct the mathematical claim before ranking the choices.

1. List every hypothesis, including domains, regularity, parameter ranges, endpoint exclusions, symmetry, and dimension assumptions.
2. State the expected conclusion type: implication or equivalence, existence or universality, containment or equality, bound or asymptotic statement.
3. Record the exact quantifiers, inequality direction, constant dependencies, endpoint status, and equality conditions you can derive or reliably recall.

Audit each option against that contract.

- Normalize logical differences explicitly: implication versus equivalence, subset versus equality, existential versus universal, strict versus non-strict, limsup versus liminf versus limit, and uniform versus case-dependent bounds.
- Split any option containing multiple formulas, regions, bounds, side conditions, or words such as “and” or “moreover” into separate claims.
- Reject the whole option if any required clause fails; a correct main idea does not rescue a false refinement.
- When two choices look equivalent, calculate the endpoint, parity, or exceptional cases instead of choosing by wording or apparent strength.

Track evidence for each option as one of:

- disproved by a definition, calculation, counterexample, or hypothesis mismatch;
- not disproved;
- positively established from the hypotheses or an exact theorem statement.

Use boundary examples, symmetry, scaling, parity, dimensions, and equality cases to eliminate claims only to the extent they actually test. Never treat the last unrefuted option as proved merely because the others failed.

Before choosing, positively verify every essential clause of the proposed answer. Do not select a familiar-sounding theorem, the sharpest-looking bound, or the cleanest formulation unless its full hypothesis-to-conclusion contract matches. If exact recall is uncertain, do not invent missing theorem details; rely only on concrete deductions and clearly identified logical mismatches.
